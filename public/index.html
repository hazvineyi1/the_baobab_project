Why there's no "aunt," "uncle," or "cousin"

English kinship is descriptive — it names an exact biological link, so every relative gets a distinct label. Shona kinship is classificatory — it groups relatives by generation, side of the family, and seniority, so whole categories of people share one term.

Your parents' generation

Babamukuru — father's older brother (a senior "father")
Babamunini — father's younger brother (a junior "father")
Amaiguru — mother's older sister (a senior "mother")
Amainini — mother's younger sister (a junior "mother")
Tete — father's sister, any age (her own distinct, senior role)
Sekuru — mother's brother, any age (his own distinct, respected role)

Your own generation

Children of your father's brothers or your mother's sisters are your siblings — Mukoma/Munin'ina if the same sex as you, Hanzvadzi if not — never "cousins." Children of your father's sister or your mother's brother are cross-cousins, and the terms skew sideways instead: you and your mother's-brother's children call each other Sekuru / Muzukuru; you and your father's-sister's children call each other Muzukuru / Sekuru the other way.

Deeper down the tree

That sideways skew doesn't stop at first cousins. Traditionally your mother's whole line of brothers, and their sons, and their sons' sons, keep being called Sekuru no matter how many generations pass — and the mirror line, through a father's sister's descendants, keeps being called Muzukuru. This tool applies that rule automatically once your tree grows past first cousins.

Elders and in-laws

Sekuru / Ambuya — both grandfathers / both grandmothers, and beyond
Muzukuru — grandchildren, nieces, nephews, and further descendants
Tsano — wife's brother
Muramu — wife's sister
Mukuwasha / Muroora — son-in-law / daughter-in-law
Murume / Mukadzi — husband / wife

Totem — Mutupo / Chidao

Every Shona person also carries a mutupo (totem, often an animal — Shumba/lion, Nzou/elephant, Shava/eland, Moyo/heart, Soko/monkey, and many more) plus a linked praise name, or chidao. It passes down the father's line only — you take your father's totem, not your mother's — which is exactly why it maps onto the same patriline that "Baba," "Sekuru," and "Muzukuru" already trace. Sharing a totem marks two people as belonging to the same clan even generations apart with no traceable link on paper, and marrying within your own totem is traditionally avoided. There's a field on each card to record it as your tree grows.

Relationships beyond blood and marriage

Sahwira — a chosen, non-blood ceremonial friend, often bound to a specific family for generations; carries real duties at funerals and other rites of passage
Chiramu — the licensed, joking familiarity between a man and his wife's younger sisters (muramu)
Roora — bride-wealth paid by the groom's family to the bride's; it's the ceremony where Tete and Sekuru formally exercise their negotiating roles, so it's woven into why those two terms carry real authority rather than just naming a relative

Usage shifts by clan, region, and family — and families disagree on details like whether cousin-seniority follows the parents' birth order or the cousins' own ages (this tool defaults to the parents' order; use the ▲▼ arrows to re-rank siblings if yours works differently). Treat this as a solid starting frame, not the final word — refine it together as your tree grows.
